# Coffee Chat Question Bank

> Print this, or keep it open on a second screen. **Reading from a list is not awkward** — it
> reads as prepared. Going blank at minute four is what is awkward.
>
> Pick 5-6 before the call. You will use three.

---

## Opening (minutes 0-4)

Small talk is a handshake protocol, not a waste of time. Two minutes.

- How is your week going?
- How long have you been in [city]?
- Is [company] office-based or are you remote these days?
- Thanks for making time — I know [day of week] can be busy.

Then hand it over: **"So how did you end up at [company]?"** This is the single best opening
question in existence. Everyone can answer it, most people enjoy it, and it usually contains
three threads you can pull on for the next ten minutes.

---

## Their path

- How did you end up at [company]?
- What did your route into [field] look like? Was it what you expected?
- What made you move from [previous company] to here?
- Was there a point where the direction changed for you?
- What do you wish you had known when you were at my stage?

---

## Their work — where the real information is

- What are you actually working on right now?
- What is the hardest problem your team is dealing with at the moment?
- What does a normal week look like — how much of it is actually coding?
- What is the tech stack, and what would you change about it?
- What is something about the work that would surprise someone outside the company?

---

## The team and the company

- How is the team structured? Who does what?
- What is the engineering culture like day to day?
- What is different about working there than you expected before you joined?
- How does work get decided — who chooses what gets built?
- How much autonomy do people have on their own projects?
- What is the onboarding like for someone joining now?

---

## Advice questions — people genuinely enjoy these

- If you were where I am now, what would you spend your time on?
- What makes someone stand out on your team?
- What do you look for when you interview people?
- What is the most common way candidates for roles like this fall short?
- Is there a skill you think is underrated in this field right now?

---

## Industry and direction

- Where do you think [field] is going in the next couple of years?
- Is there anything the industry is excited about that you think is overrated?
- What are you learning at the moment?
- Are there people, blogs, or communities you follow that you would recommend?

## The two asks — end of the conversation

Both, in this order, both softly. Written at the bottom of your list so you do not forget them.

**1. The compounding one — ask this every single time:**

> "Given my background, is there anyone else you think I should be talking to?"

Variants:
- "Is there someone on a different team whose work you think I'd find interesting?"
- "Who else at [company] would have a useful perspective on this?"

An introduction from them has a near-100% response rate. This question is why one contact
becomes five.

**2. The direct one — late, after genuine interest in their work:**

> "Is your team hiring at the moment, or do you know of anything opening up?"

Variants:
- "If something did open up on your team, what would the process look like?"
- "Is there a good way to hear about roles before they go up publicly?"

## If a referral comes up

**They offer:** take it, and make it trivially easy for them.

> "That would be really helpful, thank you. I'll send you the job link, my resume, and three
> bullet points on why I fit — so you can just forward it without having to write anything."

Then actually send it, same day. People who offer sincerely often do not follow through simply
because it became work.

**They do not offer, but the conversation went well:** it is fine to ask, once, gently.

> "If you think it'd be appropriate, I'd really value a referral for the [role] posting — but no
> pressure at all, and I completely understand if you'd rather not for someone you've just met."

That last clause matters. A referral carries their reputation, and acknowledging that makes it
much easier to say yes.

## Questions to avoid

| Do not ask | Why |
|---|---|
| Anything answered on their website | Signals you did not prepare |
| "What does [company] do?" | Same, worse |
| Salary, in a first chat | Too early. It is fine in a second conversation or with a peer |
| "Do you have any openings?" *as an opener* | Turns a conversation into a transaction in the first minute |
| "Can you refer me?" from a stranger | The single most common way these fail |
| "What's it like being a [demographic] in tech?" to a stranger | Unless they raised it |
| Anything that leads them to criticise their employer | Puts them in an awkward position |

## In-conversation reminders

- **They talk 70% of the time.** Count it if you have to
- **React before asking the next question.** "Huh, we hit exactly that —" then the question.
  Otherwise it is an interrogation
- **Ask one more question than feels natural.** The second follow-up is where the good material is
- **Let silence sit two seconds.** People often fill it with the most useful thing they say
- **Take light notes**, and say you are: "mind if I take a few notes?" It is flattering, not rude
- **Watch the clock.** You asked for fifteen minutes. At thirteen, say "I said fifteen minutes and
  I want to respect that — is it alright if I ask one more thing?" Respecting the time you asked
  for is a large part of why people say yes to a second conversation
