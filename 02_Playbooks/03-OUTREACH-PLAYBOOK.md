# Outreach Playbook

> **Never apply to a target company without reaching out to a human there first.**
>
> This is the highest-leverage activity in the entire search and the one most likely to be
> silently skipped, because it is uncomfortable and has no deadline. Everything here exists to
> lower the activation energy of sending one message to one stranger.

---

## Why this is the whole game

Public postings are saturated. Three hundred to a thousand applicants, a ~5% human-read rate, and
a filter tuned to reject. You cannot win that on merit, because merit is not what is being
measured at that stage.

The people who consistently land well do not win that competition — they **avoid it**. They were
already in conversation with someone inside before the role went up. In the mentor conversations
behind this repo the pattern was unanimous and slightly absurd: the best jobs were not applied
for. One role was never posted publicly at all — the company called someone they had spoken to
months earlier. Another came from asking a stranger for a coffee chat.

> "Talk to people you would like to be like. Talk to them even if you feel uncomfortable or out
> of place doing so, and add them to your contacts. That is the only way you will reach where you
> want to be."

Reframe it, because the framing is what makes it hard: **you are not asking for a job.** You are
asking someone to talk about their own work for fifteen minutes. That request has a much higher
acceptance rate than it feels like it does, because most people like being asked about what they
do and remember being where you are.

## Who to contact

In priority order:

| Rank | Who | Why | Response rate |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **One level above you, on the team you would join** | Knows about openings, is listened to on referrals, remembers being where you are | Highest |
| 2 | **Anyone with a shared attribute** — school, hometown, previous employer, niche community | The shared thing carries the opener. Alumni is the strongest | Very high |
| 3 | **The engineering manager for that team** | Lower response, much higher value when it lands | Low-medium |
| 4 | **Recruiters** | Good for process and timelines, weak for advocacy | Medium |

**Aim slightly higher than your own level, not dramatically higher.** A senior engineer will
answer a new grad. A VP usually will not, and if they do, they forward you to the senior engineer
you should have contacted first.

**Target: 2-3 contacts inside each Tier A and B company within 6 weeks.**

### Finding them

- **LinkedIn people search** — company filter plus title filter. Add your school to the filter
  first and work through those before anyone else
- **Alumni tools** — your school's LinkedIn alumni page, alumni directory, department lists
- **Conference talks, engineering blogs, GitHub, open-source contributions** — anyone who has
  published something has told you exactly what to open with
- **Corporate email pattern** — `firstname.lastname@company.com` is the most common format.
  Verify before sending in volume; a bounce is a wasted contact
- **Email-finder tools** — Apollo and similar browser extensions resolve a professional email
  from a LinkedIn profile. Free tiers are usually enough at this volume

## When to send

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| **Days** | Tuesday and Thursday |
| **Window** | 10:00 - 11:00 AM *their* local time |
| **Exact minute** | **Random.** 10:24, not 10:00 or 10:30 |
| **Attachment (email only)** | Resume, always |

Mid-morning Tue/Thu is when people are at their desk, through the Monday backlog, and not yet in
Friday mode. The random minute matters more than it sounds — messages arriving at 10:00 and 10:30
read as scheduled bulk sends, and people pattern-match on that.

**Prepare the batch the night before.** The block is for sending, not composing. Composing during
the block is how the block becomes twenty minutes of staring at a blank message box and then
closing the tab.

## Channel: LinkedIn vs email

| | LinkedIn connection + note | Cold email |
|---|---|---|
| **Best for** | Peers, engineers, anyone whose email you cannot get | Anyone whose address you can resolve |
| **Length limit** | ~300 characters — this is a feature | 150-200 words |
| **Response rate** | Lower per message, near-zero friction | Higher per message, more work per send |
| **Attachment** | No | Yes — always attach the resume |

**Run both.** LinkedIn for volume and for people you cannot reach otherwise; email for the ones
worth ten minutes of research.

**A connection request with no note does not count** as contact under
[Rule 1](../00_Start_Here/THE-RULES.md). It is invisible. Twenty accepted connections with no
notes produce nothing; three notes produce a conversation.

## The LinkedIn connection note

~300 characters. Full template set with variants:
[../03_Templates/linkedin-connection-note.md](../03_Templates/linkedin-connection-note.md).

The base:

> Hi [Name] — I am [your name], [one-line who you are]. I saw you are working as [their role] at
> [company], which is exactly the kind of work I am moving toward. I would love to hear about
> your experience there. Would you be open to connecting?

Four parts, and each is doing a job:

1. **Who you are** — one line, concrete, not a pitch
2. **Why them specifically** — their role, their company, their work. This is the part that
   proves you are not running a script, and it is the part people skip
3. **What you want** — to hear about their experience. **Not a job. Not a referral.** Asking a
   stranger for a referral is the single most common way this fails
4. **A low-cost ask** — "open to connecting" costs them one click

**Do not ask for a job in the first message.** You are opening a relationship, not making a
transaction. The job conversation happens in message three, or in the coffee chat, or in six
weeks when they see a req open and remember your name.

## The cold email

Three paragraphs, 150-200 words, resume attached.
[Full templates](../03_Templates/cold-email.md).

**Paragraph 1 — the connection.** What is shared? Same school, same field, a mutual contact, a
talk they gave, a post they wrote. *"Always good to see another [school] grad working in ML."*
If there is genuinely nothing shared, say what specifically drew you to their work — but look
harder first, because there usually is something.

**Paragraph 2 — you, then them.** Two sentences on your background and specialization, with one
concrete achievement. Then what *they* are doing and why it interests you. The order matters:
establish you are worth talking to, then show you have paid attention to them.

**Paragraph 3 — the ask, with your availability offered.** *"Let me know if you have time in the
next few weeks for a quick chat — I am usually free Tuesday and Thursday mornings, but happy to
work around your schedule."* Offering availability removes a scheduling round-trip. Proper
signature with your LinkedIn.

**Subject lines that work** — specific, low-key, no hype:

- `[School] grad — quick question about [team] at [company]`
- `Question about your work on [specific thing]`
- `Fellow [city] [role] — 15 minutes?`

## Follow-ups

**Most replies come from a follow-up, not the first message.** People are busy, your message
arrived during a bad hour, they meant to answer and forgot. None of that is rejection.

| # | When | What |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 7 days after the original | Short. Reply in the same thread. Add one new thing — an article, a question, a development at their company |
| 2 | 14 days after the original | Shorter. "Still interested if you have time — no worries if not, I know it is busy." |
| — | After that | Mark `cold`. Move on |

**Two follow-ups, then stop** ([Rule 7](../00_Start_Here/THE-RULES.md)). Two is persistence and
genuinely is not annoying. Five costs you the contact permanently, and the world of people who
work at your target companies is smaller than it looks.

A `cold` contact is not dead — it is dormant. Comment on their posts, and re-approach in three
months with an actual reason ("I saw your team shipped X").

[Full sequences](../03_Templates/follow-up-sequence.md).

## What to do when they reply

**They said yes to a chat.** Go to [the coffee chat playbook](05-COFFEE-CHAT-PLAYBOOK.md).
Send the calendar invite the same day.

**They answered but did not offer a chat.** Reply with a genuine follow-up question about what
they said. One exchange is a relationship in its earliest form; two exchanges and you can ask for
fifteen minutes.

**They said no, or "not right now".** Thank them, genuinely and briefly. Stay connected. A "not
right now" in August is a completely different answer in January, and people remember who was
gracious about it.

## The two goals in every conversation

Every conversation is trying to produce one of these. Ask both, in this order:

1. **"Given my background, is there someone else you think I should be talking to?"**
   This is the compounding one. An introduction from an insider has a near-100% response rate,
   and the next conversation is better-informed than this one. **This question is why one
   contact turns into five.**

2. **"Is your team hiring, or do you know of anything opening up?"**
   The direct signal. Ask it *late*, after you have been genuinely interested in their work for
   fifteen minutes.

If they point you elsewhere, that is a success, not a deflection.

## The funding play

Companies that just raised are hiring against a specific problem the investors funded. The window
is short and the applicant pool has not formed yet.

**Where to watch:** Crunchbase, Hacker News / YC announcements, TechCrunch, local tech press, and
the company's own LinkedIn.

**The message:**

> I have been following [company] for a while. I saw you raised your [Series X] from [investor] —
> congratulations. I work on [your specialization]; investors fund a round like this to solve
> [the specific problem], and with it you can now [the thing that unlocks]. Are you hiring for
> growth on that side — people who can work on those specific problems?

**What makes it land:** they must believe you are thinking at the **company's** level rather than
asking for a job. You are describing the problem they are trying to solve and demonstrating you
have done it. That reads as a peer, not an applicant.

**Then be patient.** Build the relationship first. After a few weeks: *"It was good talking with
you — could you point me to the right person about this role?"* Leading with "are you hiring"
throws the advantage away.

## Track everything

`04_Trackers/outreach.csv`, at send time ([Rule 6](../00_Start_Here/THE-RULES.md)).

Log the **seniority tier** of every contact. Response rates differ enormously between recruiter,
IC, senior IC, and manager, and after 40 contacts you will be able to see which tier answers you.
That one number changes where you spend your Tuesdays.

**Privacy:** initials plus role plus company, not full names and email addresses. This repo may go
public, and a public list of people being cold-emailed is a problem for them.

### What to review monthly

- **Response rate by seniority tier** — who actually answers you
- **Response rate by channel** — LinkedIn note vs email
- **Response rate by opener** — alumni vs their-work vs funding
- **Time to reply** — sets a realistic follow-up cadence

A 0% response rate after 30 contacts is not "networking does not work". It is one of: wrong
seniority tier, no genuine shared connection in the opener, asking for too much in message one,
or a message that reads like a template. All four are fixable, and the crew can diagnose which
in ten minutes by reading five of your sent messages.

## On the discomfort

Most people are bad at this and hate it. That is exactly why it works — the people who do it are
competing against a much smaller field than the people who only apply.

What actually helps:

- **It is 300 characters.** The realistic worst case is that a stranger ignores you, which is
  also the current state of the world
- **Batch it.** Five messages in one block is easier than one message on five days. The dread is
  per-session, not per-message
- **Separate composing from sending.** Draft the night before, when it is abstract. Send in the
  block, when it is mechanical
- **Track sends, not replies.** You control sends. Making your week's success depend on other
  people's inboxes is how the habit dies in week three
- **Let the crew send the first ones.** If a crew member is stronger at this, have them draft
  your first three. It is on the trade board in [ROSTER.md](../01_Crew/ROSTER.md) for a reason

And it is trainable — see [charisma and small talk](07-CHARISMA-AND-SMALL-TALK.md). Nobody is
born able to do this. It is a skill with a practice curve like any other.
