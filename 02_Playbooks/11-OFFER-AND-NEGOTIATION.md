# Offer and Negotiation

> The highest hourly rate you will ever earn. A twenty-minute conversation can be worth several
> thousand a year, compounding through every future raise and every future job, because your next
> offer is anchored on this one.
>
> Most people do not negotiate at all. That is the entire reason there is room to.

---

## Before any number is discussed

**Know your numbers before the first recruiter call**, because you will be asked in that call and
you will not have time to research mid-sentence.

| Number | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Market range** | What this role, at this level, in this market, actually pays |
| **Target** | What you are aiming for. Upper-middle of the market range |
| **Walk-away** | Below this you decline. Know it in advance, in writing, in your own notes |

**Where to research:** Levels.fyi (strongest for tech), Glassdoor, Blind, Indeed salary
estimates, local salary surveys, and — best of all — **asking in coffee chats**. People are
surprisingly willing to discuss ranges for a role in general terms, especially peers.

**Total compensation, not base salary.** Base, bonus, equity (and its vesting schedule and
realistic value), signing bonus, pension or retirement matching, health coverage, PTO, learning
budget, relocation, remote flexibility. A higher base with worse everything-else is often a worse
offer.

## The screening question

It arrives in the recruiter call: *"what are your salary expectations?"*

**Deflect once, then give a range.**

> "I would like to understand the scope a bit better before putting a number on it. Do you have a
> range budgeted for the role?"

If they answer, you have learned a great deal at no cost. If they push back:

> "Based on what I have seen for this kind of role in [market], I have been looking in the range
> of [X to Y]."

**Rules:**

- **A range, never a single number.** Set the bottom of your range at your target, because they
  will hear the bottom
- **Never state your current or previous salary.** In many places asking is illegal; everywhere,
  answering anchors you to your past rather than to the market. "I would rather focus on the
  value of this role" is a complete answer
- **Do not undersell to seem safe.** A number well under their band signals that you have
  misjudged the level, which costs you more than it saves

## The timing rule

**Negotiate at the end. Specifically, the last 10-15 minutes of the final interview, or after a
written offer arrives.**

Why timing dominates everything else in negotiation:

- Early, you are one of several candidates and a difficult number is a reason to move on
- Late, they have chosen you, run a process, spent internal time, and told people they found
  someone. Replacing you is expensive
- **Withdrawal at that point is rare.** The realistic worst case for a polite, well-researched
  ask is "no, that is our best offer" — which leaves you exactly where you started

**This is also when accessibility needs, accommodations, remote arrangements, and start dates get
raised.** Same logic. Raised early they can become objections; raised late they are logistics.

## When the offer arrives

**1. Thank them, and do not accept on the call.**

> "Thank you, I am really pleased. Could you send the details in writing? I would like to review
> it properly and come back to you within a couple of days."

Enthusiasm plus a pause. Never accept in the moment — the moment is designed to make you accept.

**2. Take 2-5 days.** Standard and expected. Anyone pressuring you to decide in an hour is telling
you something about the company.

**3. Read the whole thing.** Equity vesting schedule and cliff, bonus structure and whether it is
guaranteed, notice period, non-compete, IP assignment, relocation clawbacks.

**4. Tell any other companies in your pipeline.** A live offer with a deadline is the strongest
possible reason for another company to accelerate. This is completely normal and every recruiter
expects it.

## The ask

Once, clearly, with a reason. Not haggling.

> "I am genuinely excited about this role and I want to work with your team. Based on my research
> for this role in [market], and given [specific thing you bring], I was hoping we could get the
> base to [number]. Is there flexibility there?"

Three parts:

1. **Enthusiasm first** — you are not threatening to leave, you are trying to say yes
2. **A justification that is not about your needs** — market data and what you bring, never rent
   or loans. Your costs are not their problem; your market value is
3. **A specific number** and a direct question

**Then stop talking.** The silence after the ask belongs to them. Filling it is how people
negotiate against themselves.

**Ask for roughly 10-20% above the offer** if the offer is at or below market. Anchoring high but
not absurdly is what creates room to land in the middle.

## If base is fixed

Very often it genuinely is — bands are real, especially at large companies and for new grads.
That is not the end of the conversation:

- **Signing bonus** — the most flexible line item in almost every offer
- **Equity** — often has more room than base
- **Start date** — later start, or an earlier first review
- **Guaranteed first-year bonus**
- **Title** — affects your next role and your next negotiation more than most people realise
- **Remote or hybrid arrangement**
- **Learning budget, conference budget, hardware**
- **An earlier salary review** — "can we revisit at six months rather than twelve"
- **Extra PTO**
- **Relocation support**

Ask for two or three of these, not eight. A list of eight reads as difficult.

## Things to avoid

- **Inventing a competing offer.** It is checkable, and being caught ends the offer
- **Negotiating over multiple rounds.** One clear ask, one response, then decide. Coming back
  three times damages the relationship you are about to start
- **Accepting and then continuing to interview elsewhere.** Reneging is a small industry with a
  long memory
- **Apologising through the whole conversation.** Negotiating is expected. Recruiters negotiate
  for a living and are not offended by it
- **Making it about your personal circumstances.** Market value only

---

## When there is no leverage

Sometimes the answer really is a fixed band, no competing offer, and a deadline you need to meet.
It happens, particularly for first roles.

**Take the job.** A first offer taken without much negotiation is not a failure — it is a start,
and the next negotiation is much stronger because you are employed and no longer negotiating from
need.

**But still ask once.** The realistic downside is "no". Even a "no" tells you how the company
handles being asked for something, which is useful information about a place you are about to work.

## Crew angle

- **Rehearse the ask out loud with a crew member.** It sounds completely different spoken than
  read, and the first time you say a number aloud should not be to the recruiter
- **Share ranges within the crew.** Salary secrecy benefits employers, full stop. Two people
  targeting similar roles who share numbers are both better informed — this is exactly the kind
  of thing the confidentiality clause in the [charter](../01_Crew/CREW-CHARTER.md) protects
- **Have someone read the offer letter.** A second pair of eyes catches the vesting cliff, the
  relocation clawback, and the "bonus at company discretion"
- **Log the outcome** — what you asked for, what you got, what worked. That is the note that
  makes the crew's next negotiation better, including your own
