# Networking Events

> Meetups, conferences, hackathons, career fairs, alumni nights, tech talks.
>
> **The one tactical rule: stay until the end.** The most valuable conversations of the night
> happen after the formal part finishes, in the thinning room, when the people worth meeting are
> finally free and the competition for their attention has gone home.

---

## Why events are worth a Saturday

An event compresses what cold outreach does slowly. A cold email is a 10-20% shot at a reply from
one person. Two hours in a room can produce five real conversations with a near-100% "response
rate", because you are already talking.

And **in-person contacts convert far better than cold ones.** A LinkedIn request that says "great
talking with you at [event] on Thursday" is accepted almost every time, and the person actually
remembers you. That single line changes a cold contact into a warm one permanently.

---

## Which events to go to

In rough order of value for a job search:

| Type | Why | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|
| **Industry meetups** (local tech, language/framework groups) | Working engineers, recurring, low pressure, you become a regular | Can be very junior-heavy |
| **Company tech talks / open houses** | Employees of a target company, in a room, expecting to be talked to | Often thinly disguised recruiting — fine, use it |
| **Alumni events** | The shared-school opener is already in play | Skews to whoever shows up |
| **Conferences** | Density and range | Expensive; look for student, volunteer, or scholarship rates |
| **Hackathons** | You build alongside people, which is a much stronger bond than a conversation | Time-expensive |
| **Career fairs** | Recruiters, explicit hiring intent | Lowest-quality contacts. Everyone there is a resume in a stack |
| **Online events / virtual meetups** | Zero travel, sometimes global | Very low conversation quality. Chat is not a conversation |

**Where to find them:** Meetup, Eventbrite, local tech association newsletters, university
department events, target companies' own event pages, LinkedIn events, and community Discord and
Slack servers in your field.

**Pace:** one to two a month is sustainable and enough. Ten a month with no follow-up is worse
than two a month worked properly.

## Before

- **Check the attendee or speaker list** if there is one. Pick 2-3 people you want to meet and
  know one thing about each
- **Prepare your 20-second answer** to "so what do you do?" — see below
- **Set a target:** three real conversations, not thirty business cards. Three is achievable and
  leaves you with three follow-ups you will actually send
- **Bring:** phone charged (LinkedIn QR code is faster than cards), a couple of cards if your
  field still uses them, and something to write notes on
- **Eat first.** Trying to hold a plate, a drink, and a conversation is a bad time

---

## The 20-second introduction

You will say this fifteen times. Have it ready so it costs you nothing.

**Structure:** who you are → what you work on → what you are moving toward → a hook back to them.

> "I'm [name], I just finished a CS masters — my work was on computer vision, building a
> monitoring pipeline that runs on live video. I'm moving toward ML engineering roles. What about
> you, what do you work on?"

**Hand the conversation back.** The question at the end is the whole trick — it turns a statement
into a conversation and takes the pressure off you immediately.

Keep it under 20 seconds. Longer and you are pitching, and people disengage from pitches.

## During

### Arriving

- **Get there early.** A room of twelve people is dramatically easier to enter than a room of
  eighty with established clusters. Arriving early is the single best trick for anyone who finds
  events hard
- **Talk to whoever is also alone.** They are relieved. Everyone standing by the food table alone
  wants exactly what you want
- **Open groups of three or more**, never a pair — a pair is usually mid-conversation. A group
  of three has a natural gap
- **The universal opener** is the event itself: "How did you hear about this one?" / "Have you
  been to these before?" / "What brought you out tonight?" That is all you need

### Talking

- **Ask about them first.** Same 70/30 ratio as a coffee chat
- **Follow the interesting thread**, do not run a checklist
- **Be honest about looking.** "I'm looking for [role] right now" is completely normal at an
  event — that is what events are for. What is not normal is asking someone to refer you within
  four minutes of meeting
- **Quality over quantity.** Three real conversations beat fifteen introductions

### Leaving a conversation gracefully

You need this, or you will get stuck with one person for ninety minutes.

> "It was really good to meet you — I'm going to grab another drink and say hi to a few people,
> but let's connect on LinkedIn."

Then actually connect, right there, on your phone. Ninety percent of event contacts evaporate
because the connection happens "later" and later never comes.

## Stay until the end

**The rule this playbook exists for.**

Most people leave when the formal programme finishes. That is when it gets good:

- **Speakers become available.** During the event they are surrounded. At the end they are
  packing up and often genuinely free to talk. The speaker is usually the most senior and most
  connected person in the room
- **Organisers are worth knowing.** They know everyone in the local scene, they know who is
  hiring, and they are the highest-leverage single contact at any recurring event
- **Everyone left is a self-selected group** — the people who are actually there for the people
  rather than the free food
- **The clusters break up.** The tight circles that were impossible to join at 7pm are loose at
  9pm

Staying the last thirty minutes routinely produces more than the previous two hours. Plan your
transport around it so you are not the person watching the clock.

## After — where events are actually won or lost

**Within 24 hours, and this is not optional:**

1. **LinkedIn request with a note** to everyone you spoke to:
   > "Great meeting you at [event] last night — really enjoyed the conversation about [specific
   > thing]. Would like to stay in touch."
   Near-100% acceptance rate, and the specific detail makes you memorable.
2. **Log them** in `04_Trackers/outreach.csv` with source `event`
3. **Note the specific thing** you talked about. In six weeks you will not remember, and that
   detail is what makes your next message land
4. **Follow up on anything offered.** "Send me your resume" and "I'll introduce you to X" have a
   48-hour shelf life before they quietly expire

**A week later:** if a real conversation happened, ask for a proper coffee chat. Event
conversations are shallow by nature — noisy room, short attention. The chat is where it becomes
useful.

**The uncomfortable truth: most people do none of this.** They attend, they have decent
conversations, they go home, and nothing happens. The follow-up is the entire value of the event,
and skipping it makes the whole evening a social outing.

## If events are hard for you

They are hard for a lot of people. Some of what helps:

- **Arrive early.** Repeated because it is the highest-value adjustment available
- **Go with a crew member.** Two people is easier than one — but split up for part of it, or you
  will talk only to each other all night
- **Set a floor, then leave.** "Three conversations then I can go home" is a much better contract
  with yourself than "network for two hours"
- **Volunteer.** Registration desk, badges, setup. You get a role, a reason to talk to every
  single attendee, and structured conversation. It is the best hack for anyone who finds
  unstructured mingling hard
- **Ask questions during Q&A.** Say your name. Several people will approach *you* afterwards,
  which inverts the entire problem
- **Recurring beats one-off.** The same monthly meetup four times means the fourth one is walking
  into a room where people know you. That is far easier than four different events

---

## Crew angle

- **Go together**, split up, compare notes after
- **Introduce each other.** Being introduced is dramatically easier than introducing yourself,
  and a third-party introduction carries more weight than self-promotion: "you should talk to
  [name], they built [thing]" lands better from someone else
- **Share the event calendar** — one person finding an event benefits both
- **Do the follow-ups together** the next morning. It is the most-skipped step and it is much
  easier as a shared 20-minute block than as a solo chore
