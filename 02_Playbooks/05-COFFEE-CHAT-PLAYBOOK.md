# Coffee Chat Playbook

> Someone said yes. This is what to do with the next fifteen minutes — and with the six months
> after them, which is where the value actually is.
>
> **The rule: the more of these you do, the higher your probability of landing.** It is close to
> linear. There is no clever substitute for volume here.

---

## What a coffee chat is and is not

**It is:** fifteen to thirty minutes where someone tells you about their work, their team, and
their path, and you become a real person to them rather than a name.

**It is not:** an interview, a pitch, or a place to ask for a job. Every failure mode of a coffee
chat comes from treating it as one of those three.

**What it produces**, in rough order of how often:

1. **A person who knows you exist** at a company you care about. This is the actual product
2. **Information** you cannot get from outside — what the team is really building, what they
   screen for, when headcount opens, which manager to talk to
3. **A referral to another person.** Introductions convert at near 100%
4. **A referral to a job.** Least common per chat, and the reason volume matters

---

## Before

**Fifteen minutes of prep. Not more.**

- Read their LinkedIn — their path, how long there, what they did before
- Find one thing they made: a talk, a post, a repo, a blog entry, a product they shipped
- Skim the company's recent news
- **Write down 5-6 questions.** Actually write them. You will blank in the moment, and reading
  from a list is completely normal and reads as prepared rather than awkward
- Know your own 30-second answer to "so tell me about yourself"

**Logistics:**

- Send the calendar invite the same day they say yes. Momentum decays fast
- Their preferred medium. Offer video, phone, or in-person coffee — some people hate video calls
- **Ask for 15-20 minutes, not 30.** The smaller ask gets said yes to more often, and the chat
  regularly runs long anyway when it is going well
- Confirm the day before with one line

## During

### The shape of it

| Minutes | What |
|---|---|
| 0-2 | Small talk. Genuinely. Weather, their city, the thing on their wall |
| 2-4 | Your 30-second intro — only if they ask, which they will |
| 4-15 | **Them.** Their work, their path, their team. This is 80% of the conversation |
| 15-18 | Your two questions (below) |
| 18-20 | Thank them, and name a specific next step |

### The one ratio that matters

**They should be talking about 70% of the time.**

This is the single most common mistake. Nerves push people into pitching themselves for twelve
minutes, and the chat ends with someone who knows your resume and has no reason to remember you.
A person who spent fifteen minutes talking about their own work leaves feeling good about the
conversation, and *that feeling* is what makes them answer your message in November.

### Questions that work

Full bank: [../03_Templates/coffee-chat-questions.md](../03_Templates/coffee-chat-questions.md).

**Their path** — safe openers, always work:

- How did you end up at [company]?
- What did your route into this look like? Was it what you expected?

**Their work** — where the good information is:

- What are you actually working on right now?
- What is the hardest problem your team is dealing with at the moment?
- What does a normal week look like — how much is coding versus everything else?

**The team and the company:**

- What is different about working there than you expected before you joined?
- How is the team structured? Who does what?
- What is the engineering culture like day to day?

**Advice — people love being asked this:**

- If you were where I am now, what would you spend your time on?
- What do you wish you had known two years earlier?
- What makes someone stand out on your team?

**Avoid:** anything you could have googled, salary in a first chat, and "do you have any openings"
as an opening question.

### The two asks, at the end

Both, in this order, both softly:

1. **"Given my background, is there anyone else you think I should be talking to?"**
   The compounding question. An intro from them has a near-100% response rate and each
   conversation is better-informed than the last. **Ask this every single time.**

2. **"Is your team hiring, or do you know of anything opening up?"**
   The direct signal. Late in the conversation, after fifteen minutes of genuine interest in
   their work. By this point it reads as natural rather than as the reason you called.

If they offer a referral, take it and make it easy for them — see below.

## After

**Within 24 hours:**

- **Thank-you message.** Three sentences. Reference something specific they said — that is the
  proof you were listening
- **Write your notes** while it is fresh, into `01_Crew/members/<you>/logs/chat-<company>-<initials>.md`:
  what they said about the team, what they are hiring for, who they suggested you contact, and
  **one personal detail**. That detail is what makes your message in six weeks not read like a
  form letter
- **Log it** in `04_Trackers/coffee-chats.csv`
- **Act on any introduction immediately.** An intro is warm for about a week

**If they offered a referral:** send them, unprompted, the exact job link, your resume, and three
bullet points on why you fit. Make the referral a copy-paste job for them. People who offered
sincerely often do not follow through simply because it became work.

**The long game — this is the part that pays:**

- Comment on their posts occasionally. Low cost, keeps you visible
- Message every 2-3 months with something real: a project you shipped, an article relevant to
  what they mentioned, congratulations on a promotion
- **When you land somewhere, tell them.** Closing the loop is rare and memorable, and it converts
  a contact into a long-term professional relationship

A contact you chat with once and never speak to again is worth almost nothing three months later.
The chat is the beginning of the asset, not the asset.

## Volume

**This is a probability game and the volume is the lever.**

Each chat has a modest chance of producing a referral, an introduction, or timely information.
One chat is a nice conversation. Twenty chats over three months is a network inside your target
companies, and somewhere in those twenty is the one that turns into an interview.

| Pace | Per week | Over 3 months |
|---|---|---|
| Minimum | 1 | ~12 chats |
| Good | 2 | ~25 chats |
| Aggressive | 3-4 | ~40 chats |

Track chats held on the weekly scoreboard. Track chats *booked* too — booking is the number you
control, and it is the one that predicts next month.

## If you are bad at this

Most people are, at first. It is a learnable skill and the curve is steep early —
see [charisma and small talk](07-CHARISMA-AND-SMALL-TALK.md).

**Specific fixes for specific failures:**

| What goes wrong | Fix |
|---|---|
| You go blank | Written question list in front of you. Read from it. Nobody minds |
| You talk too much | Prepare a 30-second intro and *stop* at 30 seconds. Count it |
| It feels like an interrogation | After their answer, react before the next question — "oh that is interesting, we had the same issue with..." — then ask |
| Awkward silence | Let it sit two seconds. They often fill it with the most useful thing they say |
| You forget the two asks | Write them at the bottom of your question list. Every time |
| You freeze on the small talk | Two prepared openers is enough. "How is the weather there?" and "how long have you been in [city]?" have carried a million of these |

**Practice with the crew first.** Run a mock coffee chat where your crew partner plays the
stranger. Twenty minutes, and the first real one stops being the first one.
