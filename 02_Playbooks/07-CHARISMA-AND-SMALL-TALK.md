# Charisma and Small Talk — a training track

> Cold outreach, coffee chats, and events all fail for the same underlying reason: the social
> part is a skill nobody taught us, and most technical people have quietly decided they are just
> bad at it.
>
> **It is a skill with a practice curve, not a personality trait.** This is a training plan,
> not a read. Work one drill a week for eight weeks and the difference is large.

---

## The reframe

Charisma is not being funny, loud, or naturally smooth. In a professional context it reduces to
three things, and all three are trainable:

| Component | What it actually is |
|---|---|
| **Presence** | The other person feels you are actually listening rather than waiting to speak |
| **Warmth** | You seem glad to be talking to them |
| **Confidence** | You are not apologising for taking up space |

The single highest-leverage one is **presence**, and it is the easiest to fake into being real:
ask a follow-up question about what they just said. That is 80% of it. People consistently rate
the person who asked them two follow-up questions as the better conversationalist in the room,
regardless of what that person said about themselves.

## Small talk, mechanically

Small talk feels pointless to a lot of engineers because it carries no information. That is a
misreading of its function. **Small talk is a handshake protocol** — it establishes that both
parties are willing to be pleasant before anything of substance is exchanged. Skipping it does
not save time; it makes the substantive part harder.

### The two-minute structure

1. **Open with the shared context** — the event, the weather, the room, the commute. Low stakes
   on purpose. It is not supposed to be interesting
2. **Find a thread** — they mention a city, a project, a problem. Anything specific
3. **Pull the thread** — one follow-up question about that specific thing
4. **Match and add** — a short related experience of your own, then hand it back

That is it. The whole skill is step 3, repeated.

### Openers that always work

- "How did you hear about this?" / "Have you been to one of these before?"
- "How long have you been in [city]?"
- "What are you working on at the moment?"
- "How is your week going?" — surprisingly effective, nobody is asked this
- Anything about the immediate environment: the venue, the talk, the coffee

### The move that carries everything

**Ask one more question than feels natural.**

> Them: "I moved here from Vancouver two years ago."
> You: "What made you move?" ← *this is the whole technique*

Most conversations die because both people stop at the first answer. The second question is where
it becomes a real conversation, and it costs nothing.

### Getting out gracefully

Knowing how to leave makes you much more willing to enter.

> "It was really good talking to you — I'm going to grab a drink / say hello to a few people.
> Let's connect on LinkedIn."

No apology, no elaborate excuse. Warm, decisive, done.

## Eight-week training plan

One drill per week. Fifteen minutes a day at most. Do them in order — they escalate.

| Week | Drill | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **Talk to one stranger a day.** Barista, cashier, person in the elevator. One exchange beyond the transaction | Rebuilds the baseline that talking to strangers is safe and normal |
| **2** | **Ask one follow-up question** in every conversation you have, including with people you know | Trains the single highest-value move |
| **3** | **Voice memo your intro.** Record your 30-second "what do you do", listen, re-record until it stops making you wince | You cannot hear yourself in real time. This fixes rambling permanently |
| **4** | **Send 5 cold messages.** Volume, not quality. The goal is desensitisation | The dread is per-session and drops fast with reps |
| **5** | **Mock coffee chat with a crew member.** 20 minutes, they play a stranger at a target company. Then swap | Removes the "first one" problem before it costs you a real contact |
| **6** | **Attend one event.** Target: three conversations, then you may leave | Applies weeks 1-5 under real conditions |
| **7** | **Run a real coffee chat.** Prepared questions, 70/30 ratio, both asks at the end | The actual thing |
| **8** | **Review and repeat.** What was awkward? Pick one specific thing and drill it for another two weeks | The improvement is in the second pass, not the first |

## Specific failures and their fixes

| It goes wrong like this | Do this instead |
|---|---|
| **You go blank** | Prepared question list. Written. In front of you on a call, in your pocket at an event. Reading a question aloud is not weird |
| **You talk too much when nervous** | Time your intro at 30 seconds and stop, mid-thought if necessary. Then ask them something |
| **It feels like an interrogation** | React before you ask again. "Huh, we had exactly that problem —" then the question. Reaction, then question |
| **Silence panics you** | Count two seconds before filling it. People frequently use that gap to say the most useful thing in the conversation |
| **You over-prepare and it sounds scripted** | Prepare *topics*, not sentences. Four bullet points, not a paragraph |
| **You apologise for existing** | Delete "sorry to bother you", "I know you're busy", "this is probably stupid, but". None of it earns goodwill and all of it reads as low status. Just say the thing |
| **You cannot think of anything to say about yourself** | Have three stories ready: something you built, something that went wrong and what you did, something you are learning right now. Three stories cover almost every question |
| **You avoid it for weeks** | Stop trying to do it well. Do it badly on schedule. Frequency first, quality second — quality follows frequency and never the other way around |

## Practising with the crew

This is the drill the crew is uniquely good for, and it is nearly free.

**Mock coffee chat (20 min, every two weeks):**
- One person plays an engineer at a target company. The other runs a real chat, prepared questions
  and all
- Swap
- **Feedback rules:** one thing that worked, one thing to change. Not five. One change per session
  is what actually sticks

**Message review (10 min at the check-in):**
- Read each other's actual sent outreach messages
- You cannot see your own message clearly. Your crew partner can tell you in five seconds that
  paragraph two is about you and should be about them

**The dare (weekly):**
- Each person sets one uncomfortable social action for the other. Message a manager instead of an
  IC. Ask a question at the meetup Q&A. Stay to the end of the event
- Report at the check-in. This is exactly the kind of thing that does not happen without someone
  else expecting an answer

## On being introverted, anxious, or neurodivergent

None of this requires becoming an extrovert, and the advice does not change — the *format* does.

- **Structure is your advantage, not a crutch.** Written question lists, prepared intros, and
  scripted exits are what let you run a good conversation without improvising. Many naturally
  social people run worse coffee chats because they wing it
- **Sequential beats simultaneous.** One-on-one coffee chats over group events. Email over phone.
  Choose the format that suits you and do more of that format, rather than forcing the one that
  drains you
- **Budget the recovery.** An event costs you the evening after. Schedule around it instead of
  planning to be productive at 10pm
- **Volunteer at events** — a defined role removes the hardest part, which is unstructured entry
- **Scripts are not cheating.** Salespeople use scripts. Every template in
  [03_Templates/](../03_Templates/) is here for this reason

The goal is not to become someone else. It is to make the social layer cost you less, so you
actually do it — because the people who do it are competing in a much smaller field.
