# Target Company List

> Build 10-15 companies. Tier them. Work them.
>
> This is the first thing you do, because it is the input to everything else: who you reach out
> to, which postings matter, who you follow on LinkedIn, which events are worth a Saturday.

---

## Why a list at all

Without one, a job search is reactive — you apply to whatever appeared today, to companies you
had never heard of an hour ago, with a resume you cannot meaningfully tailor because you know
nothing about them. That is the search that takes five months.

With a list, everything compounds. The third time you see a company's name you know their stack.
By month two you know two people there. When a role opens, you are not a stranger applying — you
are a name someone recognises, applying to a team you can talk about.

**10-15 is the number.** Fewer than 8 and one hiring freeze wipes out your search. More than 20
and you cannot sustain relationships at any of them, which turns the list back into a wish list.

---

## The three tiers

| Tier | Count | What it is | Effort per company |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 3-5 | Dream-adjacent and genuinely plausible | High. Full tailoring, sustained outreach, you read their engineering blog |
| **B** | 5-7 | Strong fit, realistic, would be happy there | Medium — and this tier gets the **most total hours**, because it is where you will land |
| **C** | 3-5 | Would take it, not chasing it | Low. Fast standard applications, exempt from outreach-first |

**Tier B is the tier that matters.** The common failure is pouring everything into Tier A —
the famous names, the 2% acceptance rates — and treating B as a fallback. Invert it. Tier A is
lottery tickets you buy while doing the real work in B.

## How to build the list

### 1. Start from the JDs you already want (30 min)

Search your target role. Read 20 postings. Bookmark every one that made you think "I would
actually like that job". The company behind it goes on the candidate list.

Do this before thinking about brand names. It anchors the list in **roles you want** rather than
**logos you recognise**.

### 2. Add by adjacency

- **Competitors of the ones you like.** If company X is interesting, their three competitors have
  the same stack, the same problems, and less applicant volume
- **Their customers and suppliers.** Companies that buy the product often need people who
  understand it
- **The alumni signal.** Filter LinkedIn to your school and see where people 2-5 years ahead of
  you actually went. This is the highest-yield source on this page — those companies hire from
  your school, and every one of those people is a warm contact
- **Local ecosystem.** Tech associations, city startup lists, accelerator portfolios
- **Recently funded.** A Series A/B round is a hiring signal with a date on it. Crunchbase and
  Hacker News. [Outreach playbook has the funding play](03-OUTREACH-PLAYBOOK.md)

### 3. Score each candidate

Five questions. Three or more yes = it goes on the list.

- [ ] Do they hire people at my level, in my location or remote?
- [ ] Do I have any route in — an alum, a second-degree connection, a shared community?
- [ ] Is their stack close enough to mine that I am plausible?
- [ ] Can I name something specific about them that is not on the homepage? (If not, you have
      not researched them; do that before deciding)
- [ ] Would I be glad to be there in two years?

### 4. Tier and write it down

Into your `01_Crew/members/<you>/TARGET-COMPANIES.md`. Also add each to
`04_Trackers/target-companies.csv`.

## What "working a company" means

For every Tier A and B company, you are running four things in parallel:

| Track | What it looks like |
|---|---|
| **People** | 2-3 contacts inside. Engineers on the team you would join, one level above you. Not just recruiters |
| **Visibility** | Follow the company page. Follow those people. Comment on their posts. They should see your name repeatedly before you ever apply |
| **Intelligence** | Their engineering blog, their tech talks, their recent news. This is what turns a generic cover letter into a specific one, and a coffee chat into a conversation |
| **Postings** | On the daily sweep watchlist. Their careers page is checked directly — not every posting reaches the aggregators |

**Target: 2-3 named contacts inside every Tier A and B company within 6 weeks.** That is the
number that makes the list a real asset rather than a bookmark folder.

---

## Who to contact inside

Detailed in the [outreach playbook](03-OUTREACH-PLAYBOOK.md), but the short version, in priority
order:

1. **Someone one level above you on the team you would join.** The sweet spot. Senior enough to
   know about openings and to be listened to on a referral, close enough to remember being where
   you are. Highest response rate of any group
2. **Anyone who shares something with you.** Same school, same hometown, same previous employer,
   same niche community. Shared-school is the strongest single opener there is
3. **The engineering manager for the team.** Lower response rate, much higher value when it lands
4. **Recruiters.** Useful for process questions and timelines, weak for advocacy. They are not a
   substitute for an engineer on the team

---

## Review it monthly

- **Drop** anything with no movement in 8 weeks and no reply from anyone. Log why in the Dropped
  table — that stops you re-adding it in three months
- **Promote** from the watchlist when a trigger fires — funding, an office opening, someone you
  know joining
- **Re-tier.** A Tier C where you now know two people is a Tier B. A Tier A that froze hiring is
  a watchlist entry

The list is a living document. A list that has not changed in two months is not being worked.

---

## The trap

The list is not a permission structure. A great role at a company not on your list is still a
great role — take it. The list exists to direct effort on a slow Tuesday when you do not know
who to contact, not to veto opportunity.
