# Crew Charter

> What a crew is, who gets in, and what everyone is agreeing to.

---

## The idea

Isolation is not loneliness — it is **selective access**. Astronauts are cut off from Earth, not
from their crew. During a period of intense focus, the people who get your time are a small,
deliberate set. Everyone else waits.

A crew is 1–3 people, maybe 4. Not a group chat. Not a Discord server. A small number of people
who are running the same mission at the same time and have agreed to hold each other to it.

---

## The three functions

A crew member serves at least one of these. The best ones serve all three.

### 1. Accountability
They check in. They know your non-negotiables and they notice when you are slipping. They will
say *"you logged two outreach messages in a week where you committed to eight — what happened?"*
and not let the answer be a shrug.

This is the function most people think they want and most crews fail at, because calling someone
out feels like an attack unless you agreed in advance that it was the job. **You are agreeing in
advance. That is what this document is.**

### 2. Support
The low weeks are guaranteed. Rejections stack, outreach goes unanswered, and the search starts
feeling like proof of something about you rather than a queue with a latency. On those weeks the
crew's job is to remind you why you started and keep the machine running while your motivation is
offline.

Support does not mean agreeing with you. It means keeping you in the game.

### 3. Expertise
They know things you do not. They have been somewhere you are trying to go, or they are strong
where you are weak. One person interviews well and freezes at networking events; the other cold-
emails without blinking and falls apart in system design. That asymmetry is the whole value —
trade it deliberately.

---

## The alignment bar

This is the part people get wrong, so it is stated bluntly.

**Your crew must be aligned with the mission — not neutral, not vaguely supportive, and not
skeptical.** They believe in what you are doing. They want you to succeed. They are willing to
push you, challenge you, and hold you to your own highest standard rather than to whatever you
feel like doing on a Tuesday.

A well-meaning friend who says "don't be so hard on yourself, take a break" every week is not a
crew member. They are a nice person, and they belong in a different part of your life.

**Who qualifies:**
- Someone on the same path at the same time — the closest thing to a peer in the search
- Someone who has already done it — a mentor, a coach, someone a few years ahead
- Someone who will push you and can take being pushed back
- A family member who genuinely believes in the plan and will ask the hard question

**Who does not, regardless of how much you like them:**
- Anyone who needs the mission explained or defended more than once
- Anyone who competes with you rather than pushes you — they will withhold the good lead
- Anyone who cannot receive criticism, because the protocol runs both directions
- Anyone whose check-in you dread for reasons other than not having done the work

**The test:** if you missed your numbers this week, would you be relieved or uneasy that this
person is about to see it? Uneasy is correct. Relieved means they are not holding a bar.

---

## What each member commits to

1. **Show up to the weekly check-in.** Thirty minutes. Written half first, then talk.
2. **Log honestly.** Inflated numbers destroy the only thing that makes a crew useful. Zero is a
   fine number to report; a made-up four is not.
3. **Read the other person's half.** Every week. Not skimmed.
4. **Raise the slip.** If they missed a floor two weeks running, you say so. Directly, at the
   check-in, in those words. Letting it slide once is kindness; letting it slide twice is
   withdrawing the service they asked you for.
5. **Trade the asymmetry.** Whatever you are good at, actively teach. Do not wait to be asked —
   the person who needs the help is usually the person least likely to ask.
6. **Confidentiality by default.** Salary numbers, rejections, doubts, health, immigration
   status. None of it leaves the crew. This is not implicit; it is a term.

---

## What the crew does *not* do

- **Not therapy.** Real distress needs real support, and a job-search accountability partner is
  not it. Say so early and kindly rather than letting the check-in become the only outlet.
- **Not a competition.** Nobody's numbers are a scoreboard against the other person. If someone
  lands a job first, the crew keeps running — the person who landed becomes the expertise
  function for the one who has not.
- **Not a shared queue.** You do not have to apply to the same companies, want the same roles,
  or move at the same speed.

---

## Growth and exit

**Adding someone:** every existing member agrees. One veto is enough to stop it, no justification
required. A crew of two that works beats a crew of four that has gone quiet. Above four, the
weekly check-in stops fitting in thirty minutes and the format has to change — do not let it grow
past that by accident.

**Leaving:** say so. Someone who has gone quiet for three weeks has effectively left, and the
protocol should name that rather than pretend otherwise. People land jobs, change direction, or
run out of capacity. All fine. Silence is the only failure mode.

**A crew member who lands a job** is invited to stay in the expertise seat. They just came from
where the others are going and their information is at its most valuable in the first month after.

---

## The launch framing

When you launch — first interviews, final rounds, offer negotiation — you are launching with a
team that has been working alongside you the whole way. Not a group you assembled that morning
because you needed a referral. The value of a crew is entirely in the months of unglamorous
weekly check-ins that come before the moment anyone would call it useful.
